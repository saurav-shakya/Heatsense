## 1. CPR 2023: "How is India Adapting to Heatwaves?"

- **Scope.** Aditya Valiathan Pillai, Tamanna Dalal and co-authors at the Centre for Policy Research (March 2023) reviewed **37 HAPs: 15 state, 13 district and 9 city plans**. The plans cover 18 states [1][2][3].
- **Interventions.** Across the plans the authors found **62 distinct intervention types**, ranging from green roofs to school awareness drives [2].
- **Local thresholds.** Only **10 of 37** plans considered locally defined temperature thresholds. Most rely on generic national heatwave definitions [4].
- **Vulnerability.** Only **2 of 37** plans explicitly carry out and present a vulnerability assessment. Nearly all plans are poor at identifying and targeting vulnerable groups [3][5].
- **Funding.** Only **3 of 37** plans identified a funding source for at least some of their measures (Carbon Brief) [5]. Deccan Herald instead reports that "11 of 37 discussed funding sources" [6]. The two figures probably use different definitions, so cite "3 of 37 identified funding" as the stricter one.
- **Legal backing.** The plans have weak legal foundations. The authors recommend linking actions to sectoral laws, setting up a national HAP repository, creating clearer funding routes (a central fund, or the National Adaptation Fund on Climate Change), and building monitoring and evaluation [2][4]. Related reporting adds that heatwaves are not a notified disaster under national disaster law, and that only about eight states classify them as a state-specific disaster. Both points limit access to dedicated funds [7].
- **Evidence of impact is thin.** Pillai notes that comprehensive evaluation studies are needed and that "currently, there is limited data available" [7].

## 2. Later assessments, 2024–2026, and Delhi's plan

- **Sustainable Futures Collaborative (SFC), "Is India Ready for a Warming World?" (March 2025).** The study covered **9 cities**: Bengaluru, Delhi, Faridabad, Gwalior, Kota, Ludhiana, Meerut, Mumbai and Surat. These hold more than 11% of India's urban population [8]. It was based on **88 interviews** with officials [8].
  - All nine cities focus on immediate, short-term responses: drinking water, shifted work schedules and extra hospital capacity.
  - Long-term actions are rare and, where they exist, poorly targeted [8].
  - Mongabay's coverage says heat action "crumbles when it needs cross-department coordination" [9].
- **PLOS Climate ten-city study (desk review of 2023–24 plans plus interviews).** Officials describe coordination as active only during summer. One official said there was "no coordination after the summer months" [10].
- **PMC study of eight cities.** It names four areas that need improvement [11]:
  - "particular care for vulnerable groups"
  - preparedness of the health and social-care system
  - long-term urban planning
  - "real-time surveillance"
- **Kotharkar & Ghosh (2022) systematic review of heat-health action plans, 1995–2020.** Plans emphasise a lead agency, health-information plans and alert systems. They neglect care for vulnerable groups and long-term urban planning [7].
- **Scale of adoption (NRDC/IIPH-Gandhinagar).** Ahmedabad's 2013 plan was South Asia's first. NDMA has asked **23 heat-prone states** to prepare HAPs, and over **130 cities and districts** are reportedly working on one. These are NRDC-cited government figures [12]. New city plans followed in 2025 (Churu, and Varanasi on 16 May 2025) [12].
- **Delhi HAP 2024-25 (DDMA PDF, as excerpted by search)** [13]:
  - Activate **cooling centres** (temples, public buildings, malls) during alerts.
  - Provide shade for outdoor workers and keep night shelters open.
  - Provide **drinking water** at major points and all worksites.
  - Have employers **move outdoor work out of 1–5 pm** during alerts.
  - Agencies named: MCD, PWD, DUSIB, DJB, NDMC.
- **Delhi HAP 2025 (April 2025).** Press reports describe IMD-linked **graded yellow, orange and red alerts**, and cooling centres near footpaths and slums with chilled water [14]. Reported water-point numbers conflict: about 3,000 water coolers in one report and 11,000 water points in another. Treat both as unverified [14].
- **Implementation gap in Delhi.** A Local Samosa report says there was **one cooling zone for the whole city** (near Jama Masjid metro), and that it was largely unused. It also says the noon–3 pm ban on outdoor construction was visibly unenforced [15]. This is a single journalistic account.

## 3. WHO/WMO guidance and evidence that warning systems work

- **WMO/WHO 2015 guidance.** *Heatwaves and Health: Guidance on Warning-System Development* (WMO-No. 1142, edited by G.R. McGregor and others) describes itself as generic rather than prescriptive [16][17]. It covers:
  - vulnerability factors and heat-stress assessment
  - how to define and build a heat-health warning system (HHWS)
  - communicating warnings to stakeholders and the public
  - intervention strategies
  - evaluation of warnings and protective measures
  - seasonal and longer-term planning
  - It places the warning system *inside* a wider heat-health action plan [16][17].
- **Toloo et al. (2013), systematic review of 15 articles** [18]:
  - **6 studies** reported fewer heat deaths after a warning system was introduced, and warning systems were associated with lower ambulance use.
  - **8 studies** showed that "mere availability" of a warning system did not change behaviour.
  - **Perceived personal threat** was the main factor in acting on warnings. Cost was a barrier, especially for poorer households.
- **Systematic review of HHWS (2025, *Current Environmental Health Reports*).** Most systems rely on temperature–mortality relationships. The review recommends adding humidity, local epidemiology and morbidity outcomes. It says there is not enough evidence on household or personalised warning systems [19].
- **Ahmedabad (Hess et al., 2018).** After the HAP, about **1,190 deaths per year were avoided** (95% CI 162–2,218) in 2014–15 compared with a 2007–10 baseline [20][21]. This is a pilot before-and-after design, and the data were not disaggregated by gender or occupation [20].

## 4. Risk communication: why people ignore warnings

- **People do not see themselves as at risk.** Of these barriers, it has the strongest evidence.
  - **England, 2026 survey of 1,097 adults (Aug 2025)** [22][23]:
    - 30% had not encountered heat-health alerts. These were mostly older, low-income and less-educated adults.
    - 91% said they were willing to adapt, but only **41%** took protective action.
    - The most common reason for inaction was *not believing heat put them at risk*.
  - **Queensland survey of 547 people aged 65+.** Only **20%** saw themselves at heat risk. Those who did were **3.62 times** more likely to act on warnings [24].
  - **Earlier English data.** Only 40% of people aged 75+ and 33% of those aged 65–74 agreed that hot weather was a health risk [24].
  - **Scoping review.** 83% of seniors with high perceived risk changed behaviour on warning days, against 22% of those with low perceived risk [24].
- **Low-level alerts matter.** In England, a significant share of heat deaths occur during **lower-level alerts**. The reasons are low awareness and misunderstood alert levels [22].
- **Alert fatigue.** I found **no heat-specific study** quantifying alert fatigue. The evidence is analogous: in hospital settings, 72–99% of alarms are false and lead to overrides [25]. Treat heat-alert fatigue as a plausible risk, not an evidenced finding.
- **Access is unequal (Ahmedabad).** A study of women outdoor labourers found that illiteracy and low phone ownership create unequal access to warnings. **Verbal warnings and community engagement partly offset this** [26].
- **Trusted messengers.**
  - Greenpeace India, informal-worker groups and IMD in Delhi simplified and translated IMD alerts. They spread them through **community WhatsApp groups** and on boards with colour codes and emojis at vending carts and labour chowks, for workers who cannot read [27].
  - Mahila Housing Trust sent colour-coded heat alerts to women's phones and put them on street posters in Jodhpur [27].
- **ASHA workers.** A national training module gives ASHAs a role in heat awareness and symptom spotting [28]. In practice:
  - In several districts, ASHAs received heat instructions only in **April**, after February–March heatwaves [28].
  - Each ASHA covers 200–700 households [28].
  - In 2026, ASHAs in Mumbai were diverted to Census duty in 40–45°C heat [28].
- **Channels.**
  - WhatsApp has about **500 million users in India**. This figure recurs from 2023 to 2025 and is not a fresh official count [29].
  - Feature phones still matter. Counterpoint-cited reporting (2026) puts users at **220 million or more** [30]. IDC in 2024 put the figure at about **350 million** [31].
  - Smartphone penetration is estimated at under 50% (IDC) [31].
  - I found no Indian evaluation of IVR heat alerts.

## 5. Cooling centres

- **No outcome evidence.**
  - A CDC systematic review found **no direct evidence** that cooling centres reduce deaths or illness. Usage is hard to track [32].
  - A 2025 review found **no published studies** of the health impacts of real-world cooling centres [33].
- **What happens to the body.** A laboratory simulation found that brief air-conditioning lowered core temperature in older adults, but **the benefit was temporary** [34]. A Phoenix study recommends at least **70 minutes** of recovery time [35].
- **Barriers.** Older adults face [32][36]:
  - transport problems
  - **limited opening hours**, especially nights and weekends
  - limited mobility
  - safety concerns
  - **lack of awareness**
  - Cognitive decline also reduces people's ability to recognise danger.
- **India.** Delhi's plan lists cooling centres [13], but the one documented cooling zone was reportedly unused [15].

## 6. Buddy and check-in systems for isolated elderly people

- **Why they exist.** France's August 2003 heatwave caused about **14,800–15,000 excess deaths**. Mortality among people aged 75 and over was about 70% above baseline [37]. Most victims were community-dwelling elderly people living at home. Risk factors were lack of mobility, pre-existing illness, poor insulation and sleeping directly under the roof [37]. Isolated victims were described as "invisible" [37].
- **The response.**
  - After 2003, towns built registries of elderly people living alone, with help from Petits Frères des Pauvres. On a heat alert, volunteers and municipal staff **call and visit everyone on the list** [38].
  - Registration is voluntary, started by the person or their family [38].
  - Paris runs the **REFLEX** scheme: register by calling 3975 or on paris.fr. It was activated during recent heatwaves [38].
  - I could **not verify the name "Chalex"**; Paris's scheme appears as REFLEX.
- **Outcomes.** I found **no evaluation** that isolates the registry's effect on mortality. A European review notes that post-2003 measures focused mainly on warning systems [37].
- **Scam risk.** Fraudsters have posed as municipal social-services (CCAS) staff checking the registry [38]. Any check-in channel needs verification cues.

## 7. Heat-health data gaps in India

- **Surveillance system.** NCDC's National Programme on Climate Change and Human Health (NPCCHH) has collected heatstroke data since 2015 [39]. In 2023 it moved to the IHIP platform [40]:
  - year-round reporting from primary health centres (PHC) upward
  - daily figures from 1 March each season
  - reports cover heatstroke cases and deaths, emergency attendance, and total and cardiovascular deaths
  - each suspected heat death is to be investigated
- **Reported numbers.**
  - **2024:** 48,156 suspected heatstroke cases, 269 suspected heatstroke deaths and **161 confirmed** deaths [40].
  - **2025 (1 Mar–24 Jun):** 7,192 suspected cases and **14 confirmed deaths** (RTI data) [39].
- **Sources disagree.**
  - **2015–2022:** NCDC counted 3,812 heat deaths, NCRB 8,171 and IMD 3,436 [39].
  - **Mar–Jul 2023:** HeatWatch found 733 heatstroke deaths in media reports. The health ministry reported 360 [41].
- **Why deaths are missed.** Counts depend on hospitals, so deaths at home or misdiagnosed deaths go uncounted [39][41]. Other causes:
  - manual data entry and understaffing [39]
  - **Delhi** is among the states that have failed to submit complete data [39]
  - suspected suppression to avoid compensation claims [39]
  - death certificates that record the immediate cause rather than heat [41]
- **The true burden is far larger.** A 2026 modelling study (Frontiers in Environmental Health) estimates about **3,400 excess deaths per extreme-heat day** nationally, and nearly **30,000 for a five-day heatwave**. Official tallies are about 500–1,500 a year [41][42]. These are model estimates, not counts. The study builds on de Bont et al.'s 10-city analysis (*Environment International* 2024; 2008–19, about 3.6 million deaths) [43].
- **What cities lack.** Taken together, the sources above point to these gaps:
  - real-time ward-level morbidity (the eight-city review flags "real-time surveillance") [11]
  - vulnerability maps (only 2 of 37 HAPs have one) [3]
  - local thresholds (10 of 37) [4]
  - mortality data disaggregated by gender or occupation [20]
  - evaluation of whether plans work [7]

## Implications for product

- **Warning about heat is not the same as making people act.** Availability alone did not change behaviour [18]. In England, only 41% of adults acted on alerts [22].
  - Measure success as *actions taken*: confirmed check-ins, people reached through a caregiver, a cooling-centre visit. Delivery rates are not enough.
  - Every message should name **one concrete action, a place and a time**. For example: "Nearest free water/shade: X, open till 8 pm. Avoid outdoor work 1–5 pm" [13].
- **Personalise risk, especially for the elderly.** The main reason people ignore alerts is that they do not think heat endangers *them* [22][24]. Write messages that name the recipient's own risk ("You are 70+ / on BP medicines…"). Consider sending alerts to a caregiver or family member, not only to the elderly person.
- **Build ward-level thresholds and vulnerability layers into the officer map.** That is exactly what most Indian HAPs lack: only 10 of 37 have local thresholds and only 2 of 37 have vulnerability assessments [3][4]. It is the clearest product wedge for city officials.
- **Plan for multiple channels, not WhatsApp only.**
  - WhatsApp reaches about 500 million people [29].
  - 220–350 million people still use feature phones [30][31].
  - In Ahmedabad, low phone ownership and illiteracy cut off women workers [26].
  - So: SMS plus a voice or IVR fallback, Hindi audio, and visual or colour-coded formats [27].
  - IVR effectiveness in India is unproven, so pilot it and measure.
- **Distribute through trusted intermediaries.** Supply ASHAs, RWAs, worker unions and vendor networks with ready-to-forward Hindi/English assets and a daily ward brief [27][28]. Send pre-season training content in February, not April [28].
- **Make the elderly check-in loop a core feature.** Build a voluntary registry with buddy or volunteer calls on alert days, modelled on France's post-2003 registries and Paris REFLEX [38].
  - Add escalation (no reply → call the buddy → notify the ward officer).
  - Add anti-scam verification, given the CCAS impersonation cases [38].
  - Instrument it to produce the outcome evidence that France lacks.
- **Treat cooling centres as an operations problem.** There is no outcome evidence for them [32][33], and the main barriers are awareness, distance, hours and mobility [36]. In Delhi, the one cooling zone sat largely unused [15].
  - Show the *nearest open* centre or water point with live hours.
  - Let officers log opening and footfall, so cooling centres become measurable.
- **Fill the data gap carefully and manage alert fatigue.** Official counts miss most deaths (14 confirmed deaths in 2025 against model estimates in the thousands) [39][42]. Delhi has under-reported to NCDC in past years [39].
  - Offer officers a lightweight ward-level morbidity and incident log (ambulance calls, PHC heat cases) that can feed IHIP [40].
  - Escalate alert tiers sparingly. Heat-specific evidence on alert fatigue is missing [25], but deaths cluster at *lower* alert levels [22]. Lower-tier messages should still carry advice targeted at high-risk recipients, not be suppressed.
